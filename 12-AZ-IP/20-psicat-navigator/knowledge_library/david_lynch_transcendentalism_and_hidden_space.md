## Lynch's Hidden Space

### The Ocean of Knowingness

David Lynch describes accessing what he calls a "limitless field of pure consciousness" — an "ocean of knowingness" — through Transcendental Meditation (TM). He has practiced TM since 1973 and founded the David Lynch Foundation for Consciousness-Based Education and World Peace.

From Lynch's perspective:
- Meditation allows access to a realm "from which inspiration naturally arises"
- This realm is hidden from ordinary consciousness
- It is functional — it produces real creative output
- It is accessible through practice but not directly observable

### Films From the Subconscious

Lynch's films are famously described as emerging from the subconscious:
- "His movies seem to emerge straight from the twisted depths of his subconscious, as free from logic as your weirdest dreams" (Times-Standard)
- Eraserhead (1977): Lynch described as "a dream of dark and troubling things"
- Mulholland Drive (2001): Explores the dissolution of boundary between dream and reality
- Twin Peaks: The liminal space between worlds is the central theme
- Inland Empire (2006): Film itself becomes a non-orientable surface — scenes fold back on themselves, identities merge

### The Liminal in Lynch's Cinema

Lynch's films consistently operate in the liminal region — the space between states:
- Between waking and dreaming (Mulholland Drive)
- Between life and death (Twin Peaks)
- Between reality and fiction (Inland Empire)
- Between the industrial and the organic (Eraserhead)
- Between the conscious and the subconscious (all his work)

This liminal space has the properties of non-orientable surfaces: no clear inside/outside, self-reference that transforms the observer, boundaries that dissolve.
