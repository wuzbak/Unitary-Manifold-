### 1.1 THE MENTOR: ROY COHN

To understand Donald Trump's relationship with law and power, you must begin with Roy Cohn. This is not a metaphor or a psychological observation. It is a documented factual relationship that shaped how Trump operates across every domain of his business and political life.

Roy Cohn was, by any objective measure, one of the most morally bankrupt lawyers in American history. He was the chief counsel to Senator Joseph McCarthy during the Red Scare hearings. He represented the Genovese and Gambino crime families. His clients included Tony Salerno (Genovese boss), Carmine Galante (Bonanno boss), and a galaxy of other organized crime figures. He was a master of intimidation, extortion, character assassination, and legal obstruction. He died in 1986, disbarred for unethical conduct, but his methods did not die with him.

Trump met Cohn in 1973 at a party at Le Club in Manhattan. He was immediately attracted — not to Cohn personally, but to what Cohn represented: the ability to project raw power, to attack rather than defend, to never admit wrongdoing, to treat legal proceedings as theatrics of dominance rather than exercises in truth-finding. Trump has described the relationship as formative. Multiple biographers document that Trump absorbed Cohn's core operating principles:

- **Never settle; attack.** (Trump's subsequent record includes more than 3,500 lawsuits.)
- **Deny everything, even the obvious.** (His public record is replete with demonstrably false denials.)
- **Loyalty is one-directional; discard anyone who becomes a liability.** (Cohn himself was eventually discarded by Trump as he was dying of AIDS.)
- **Use law as a weapon, not a constraint.** (His documented legal strategy across six bankruptcy proceedings, fraud settlements, and criminal indictments is consistent with this.)

**Confidence: CONFIRMED** — Multiple biographies, Trump's own statements, court records documenting Cohn's representation of organized crime clients (Source: Wayne Barrett, *Trump: The Deals and the Downfall*, 1992; NY Supreme Court disbarment records, 1986).

### 2.2 THE RYBOLOVLEV TRANSACTION: THE SMOKING GUN OVERPAYMENT

Of all the suspicious real estate transactions in the Trump record, the Rybolovlev deal stands alone for the magnitude of the premium paid and the subsequent behavior of the buyer.

**The documented facts:**

- **2004:** Trump purchases Maison de L'Amitié, a Palm Beach estate, out of a bankruptcy auction for **$41.35 million.**
- **2008:** Dmitry Rybolovlev, a Russian fertilizer oligarch with documented connections to Russian political power, purchases the property from Trump for **$95 million** — an overpayment of approximately 130% above the purchase price in just four years, and roughly **$30 million above appraised value** at the time of sale.
- **Post-purchase:** Rybolovlev never occupies the property. He immediately subdivides it into three parcels and sells them through **shell companies** to anonymous buyers — the exact mechanism used to launder money in the Panama Papers structures.
- **2017:** Rybolovlev's private jet appears at airports in the same cities as Trump's jet on at least three documented occasions during the 2016 presidential campaign. The coincidence is noted by multiple investigators; no explanation is offered.
- **2018:** Senator Ron Wyden formally requests that the Treasury Department provide any Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) filed in connection with the Rybolovlev transaction. No public response is ever provided.
- **Mueller probe:** Special Counsel Mueller's team reviews the transaction as part of the broader Russia inquiry. No charges are filed specifically related to the Rybolovlev deal.

**What the numbers say:** In a depressed 2008 Palm Beach market (the financial crisis had cratered luxury real estate), Rybolovlev paid a price that no legitimate buyer would pay. The immediate subdivision and sale through anonymous shell companies eliminated any possibility of personal use as a motivation. The only rational economic explanation for paying 130% over purchase price and immediately selling through untraceable entities is: the transaction was not primarily about the property.

**Confidence: CORROBORATED** — Property records (Palm Beach County); Senator Wyden's formal Treasury inquiry (Senate Finance Committee, 2018); Reuters reporting; ABC News reporting.

**Potential laws violated:** Bank Secrecy Act (31 U.S.C. § 5318) if SARs were required and not filed; Money Laundering Control Act (18 U.S.C. § 1956) if proceeds were from specified unlawful activity.

### 3.2 TRUMP TOWER BAKU — DEAL WITH THE REVOLUTIONARY GUARD

The Trump Organization's aborted hotel project in Baku, Azerbaijan is one of the most explicit examples of willful blindness to Foreign Corrupt Practices Act obligations in the Trump business record.

**The deal:**

In 2012, the Trump Organization signed a management and licensing agreement to develop a luxury hotel in Baku's upscale Nasimi district. The development partner was Anar Mammadov, son of Ziya Mammadov, Azerbaijan's Transport Minister. The licensing arrangement generated millions in fees for the Trump Organization before the project collapsed in 2015.

**The documented corruption:**

U.S. diplomatic cables (classified, but widely reported on following unauthorized disclosure) describe Ziya Mammadov as "notoriously corrupt even for Azerbaijan." The cables document his family's wealth as wildly disproportionate to his government salary — a classic indicator of public corruption.

The construction contractor for the Baku project was **Azarpassillo**, an Iranian construction company. Azarpassillo's chairman, Keyumars Darvishi, is documented as a former commander in an Iranian Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) construction division. The IRGC is designated by the United States government as a foreign terrorist organization. Doing business with IRGC-controlled entities is a potential violation of U.S. sanctions law.

**The legal exposure:**

The FCPA prohibits U.S. companies from profiting from partnerships where FCPA red flags exist and due diligence has not been performed. The combination of: (a) a partner who is the son of a notoriously corrupt minister; (b) a construction contractor controlled by IRGC-connected figures; (c) an opaque financial structure that obscured the ultimate beneficiaries of payments — created a red flag environment that any competent FCPA compliance program would have flagged immediately.

Senate Foreign Relations Committee members formally wrote to the Treasury Department, the DOJ, and the FBI requesting investigations into whether the Trump Organization violated the FCPA and U.S. sanctions law through the Baku deal. No prosecution resulted.

**Why this matters:** The FCPA violation, if proven, would be a felony. More importantly, the Baku deal demonstrates the Trump Organization's operating posture toward due diligence in foreign markets: it did not conduct meaningful due diligence on its partners' corruption exposure or sanctions connections, because doing so might have prevented lucrative licensing deals.

**Confidence: CORROBORATED** — The New Yorker (Adam Davidson, "Donald Trump's Worst Deal," March 2017); U.S. diplomatic cables (reported by multiple outlets); Senate Foreign Relations Committee letter (public record); Center for American Progress analysis.

### 4.1 THE ONLY BANK LEFT

Between 2011 and 2020, Deutsche Bank was Donald Trump's **sole institutional lender in the United States**. This is not a minor footnote. This is the central financial fact of Trump's adult business life following his 1990s Atlantic City bankruptcies, and it is the thread that connects banking fraud, Russian money laundering, and the Epstein network within a single institution.

**Why Deutsche Bank was the only lender:**

Every major American bank — JPMorgan Chase, Citibank, Goldman Sachs, Bank of America — had severed their lending relationships with Trump by the early 2000s following a series of defaults and bankruptcies. By one count, Trump's companies filed for bankruptcy six times between 1991 and 2009. He defaulted on loans from Bankers Trust, Midlantic Bank, and the now-defunct Manufacturers Hanover Trust. He negotiated favorable restructurings that left creditors with pennies on the dollar.

Deutsche Bank continued lending because Deutsche Bank had its own problems. Its private wealth management division, which handled the Trump relationship, operated with weak controls and — as multiple enforcement actions eventually proved — an institutional culture that prioritized revenue over compliance.

**The loans:**

Between 2011 and 2020, Deutsche Bank extended Trump's businesses approximately **$340 million** in outstanding loans. These loans were secured by financial statements that Judge Arthur Engoron later found to be materially and systematically fraudulent.

**The suppressed Suspicious Activity Reports:**

This is the most explosive dimension of the Deutsche Bank relationship, and it is the least discussed.

Deutsche Bank's anti-money laundering compliance staff internally flagged multiple transactions by Trump entities — and by Jared Kushner's companies — and **recommended filing Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) with federal authorities** as required under the Bank Secrecy Act. In multiple instances, those recommendations were **quashed by senior bank officials**.

Senators Ron Wyden and Sherrod Brown wrote formally to Deutsche Bank: *"Why did Deutsche Bank block the filing of suspicious activity reports relating to transactions by Donald J. Trump and Jared Kushner?"* Deutsche Bank's response was opaque.

Congressional subpoenas for Deutsche Bank's Trump-related records were issued by both the House Financial Services Committee and the House Intelligence Committee. The Trump White House sought to block these subpoenas. The courts ultimately upheld Congress's right to the records, but the underlying documents — including the SARs themselves — have never been released to the public in their entirety.

**The Russian mirror-trading connection:**

During the precise years that Deutsche Bank was serving as Trump's sole lender, Deutsche Bank was also running what regulators later determined to be a $10 billion **Russian "mirror trading" scheme** — one of the most sophisticated money laundering operations ever documented at a major Western bank. The scheme worked by having Russian clients buy securities in rubles in Moscow and simultaneously sell identical securities in dollars in London, effectively converting ruble-denominated wealth into clean dollar assets that could move through the international financial system.

Deutsche Bank's 2017 settlement with the U.S. Federal Reserve, New York DFS, and UK FCA resulted in a **$630 million fine** and a finding of "serious compliance deficiencies." The mirror-trading clients were primarily wealthy Russians seeking to move capital out of Russia.

Whether the Trump lending relationship and the Russian mirror-trading operation within the same institution were operationally connected has never been officially examined. What is documented is this: Deutsche Bank was laundering Russian money while simultaneously being the only institution willing to lend to Trump. This should be investigated.

### 4.2 THE FRAUD: JUDGE ENGORON'S FINDINGS

The civil fraud case brought by New York Attorney General Letitia James against Trump, Trump Organization, and his sons Donald Jr. and Eric produced the most comprehensive judicial examination of Trump's financial practices ever conducted.

**Judge Engoron's findings (February 2024):**

Judge Arthur Engoron found, after a full bench trial, that Trump had provided **materially false financial statements to Deutsche Bank** — inflating his net worth by between **$812 million and $2.2 billion per year** over a sustained period. The false statements were used to secure favorable loan terms and insurance rates.

The specific falsifications documented in the court record include:
- Mar-a-Lago was valued at up to $739 million on Trump's financial statements. Engoron found its actual market value was between $18 million and $27.6 million — a factor of more than **26x overstatement**.
- Trump's apartment at Trump Tower was listed as 30,000 square feet on his financial statements. Its actual size is approximately 10,996 square feet — a **3x overstatement** of the physical dimensions.
- Cash and liquid assets were routinely overstated.
- Goodwill associated with the Trump brand was assigned values that were not supportable by any recognized valuation methodology.

Engoron initially imposed a penalty of approximately **$355 million**, which with statutory interest reached approximately **$454–515 million**. In August 2025, a New York appeals court found the penalty amount excessive under the Eighth Amendment and reversed the monetary award, though it **did not overturn the underlying fraud finding**. The New York Attorney General announced intent to appeal the monetary reversal to New York's Court of Appeals.

**The key takeaway:** The fraud finding itself stands. A New York court has determined, on the full evidentiary record, that Donald Trump submitted systematically false financial statements to obtain loans and favorable terms. This is not disputed. The penalty amount is being litigated. The conduct is judicially established.

**Confidence: CONFIRMED** — New York Supreme Court, *People of the State of New York v. Donald J. Trump et al.*, Decision and Order (February 2024); New York Appellate Division ruling (August 2025).

**Potential laws violated:** 18 U.S.C. § 1344 (bank fraud); New York Penal Law § 175.10 (falsifying business records, first degree); New York Executive Law § 63(12) (persistent fraud or illegality in business).

### 5.2 THE HUSH MONEY CONVICTION: 34 FELONY COUNTS

**Status: CONFIRMED — Criminal conviction, personal**

On May 30, 2024, a Manhattan jury convicted Donald J. Trump on **all 34 counts** of falsifying business records in the first degree — making Trump the **first former U.S. President to be convicted of a felony crime**.

**The scheme:**

In the weeks before the 2016 presidential election, Trump directed his personal attorney Michael Cohen to pay adult film actress Stormy Daniels **$130,000** in exchange for her silence about a sexual encounter she says she had with Trump in 2006. Trump subsequently reimbursed Cohen through a series of payments disguised in Trump Organization records as "legal fees." Each falsified record — each check, invoice, or ledger entry that mischaracterized the payment — constituted a separate felony count.

The underlying purpose of the scheme was to prevent the disclosure from influencing the 2016 election. This elevated the falsification of business records from a misdemeanor to a felony under New York law.

**Sentencing:** On January 10, 2025, Judge Juan Merchan sentenced Trump to an **unconditional discharge** — no jail time, no probation, no fine — citing the unprecedented circumstances of the defendant's return to the presidency. The 34 felony convictions remain on Trump's record. Appeals are ongoing.

**The cover-up dimension:** Michael Cohen, the man who executed the payment on Trump's behalf, was himself convicted and served jail time. The man who directed the scheme received an unconditional discharge because he had won a presidential election. This asymmetry is itself a comment on the architecture of impunity.

**Citation:** People of the State of New York v. Donald J. Trump, Manhattan Supreme Court (May 2024); Judge Merchan sentencing memorandum (January 2025).
