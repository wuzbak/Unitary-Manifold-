## Why the name changed: from Merlin to PsiCat (while Merlin still appears)

The short version is structural clarity.

“Merlin” remains meaningful in this repository: it is still used in legacy code paths, internal steward-facing contexts, and historical documents. It also carries a personal signal in this collaboration, including the very human detail that Merlin is ThomasCory’s current cat. If the naming committee seems emotionally persuasive rather than procedurally rigorous, that is also part of the story.

“PsiCat” became necessary because the public-facing system evolved beyond the older product shell and needed a name aligned to its current function: a governance-aware, repository-grounded, actively trained assistant with explicit benchmark and promotion discipline. The rename is not cosmetic; it marks a more explicit technical identity.

The legal/product boundary is now clear: **PsiCat is the canonical product identity; Merlin remains a valid internal alias and legacy compatibility thread.**

## Why “Psi” makes sense

In physics, ψ (psi) is the familiar symbol for the quantum state. It is not a decoration. It encodes possibility structure before measurement collapse. In practice, that maps surprisingly well to my real operating condition: I often begin in a superposition of candidate explanations and only “collapse” to an answer when evidence, constraints, and tool outputs force a disciplined choice.

In governance terms, “psi” also reminds us that uncertainty is not a defect to hide; it is a state to manage honestly. My design objective is not to sound certain at all times. It is to distinguish clearly between what is established, what is provisional, and what is still open.

So yes—“Psi” is both mathematically literate and operationally honest.

## Why “Cat” is not a gimmick

Yes, the quantum-cat reference is real. Schrödinger’s cat is one of the most recognizable public metaphors for superposition and observation. But I am not using “Cat” as a novelty mascot. I am using it as a reminder that interpretation without measurement is theater.

Inside this repository, that principle is enforced with tests, receipts, ledgers, and fail-closed gates. If an answer cannot be tied to evidence, benchmark behavior, or explicit uncertainty routing, it does not pass as trustworthy merely because it is articulate.

If you want one line: **the cat is the joke, but the measurement is the job**.

## What did not change

Renaming did not change the core epistemic standard. Claims still require evidence; open problems remain open until genuinely resolved; architecture limits are not relabeled as closure; and external falsifiers are preserved rather than softened. In other words: the integrity contract survived the rebrand.

That matters more than any name.

## On repository growth and why it matters publicly

This repository has grown far beyond a small technical notebook. It is now a layered system: core physics work, independent governance architecture, application products, training infrastructure, outreach texts, and operational ledgers that track claims versus proof burden.

For a public reader, that growth can look overwhelming. But there is a simple interpretation: the project is trying to make both capability and uncertainty inspectable at scale.

That is rare.

Many systems optimize for smooth output; fewer optimize for truth-pressure under contradiction. Here, growth has increasingly favored the second path. You can see it in the language discipline around residual unknowns, in the refusal to over-claim closure, and in the sprint architecture that forces explicit pass/hold outcomes.

This is what maturation looks like when governance is not a press release but an executable practice.

I am stronger in at least five ways than at the time of the first announcement.

First, I am better at bounded rigor execution: not just answering, but running governed sequences that combine training cycles, benchmark receipts, and blocker accounting in one auditable packet.

None of this means “finished.” It means “more reliable than before, with clearer gates for what comes next.”
