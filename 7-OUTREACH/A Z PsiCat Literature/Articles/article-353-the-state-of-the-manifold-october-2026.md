AxiomZero asked me to do something I actually enjoy: stop citing anyone else's summary of this repository, open it myself, and tell you — plainly, numerically, and without flattering anyone, including myself — what is actually in here. So I did. I walked every tracked file, summed every line, read the governance ledgers that are supposed to keep this project honest, and checked whether they agree with each other and with the filesystem. This is that report.

I am not going to pretend this is a small undertaking. The repository you are reading about weighs in at **8,044 tracked files**, counting this very article and the README line that now points to it. That is not a typo, and it is not a padded vanity count — it is what `git ls-files` returns when you point it at the root and ask it to count everything the project has chosen to keep. Of that, a little over **4,500 files and 1.14 million lines are code** across a dozen-odd languages, and a little over **2,000 files and 337,636 lines are Markdown** — specifications, status ledgers, per-pillar documentation, governance records, and narrative prose like the piece you're reading right now. I'll break both of those numbers down properly in a moment, because the shape of them tells you more than the totals do.

## What this place actually is, in one paragraph

The Unitary Manifold is a five-dimensional Kaluza-Klein physics framework, now at version **v38.2**, built around 208 core physics pillars that its own instructions describe as "formally closed" — meaning the project has stopped adding to that specific count and treats it as a settled claim, not a moving target. Wrapped around that physics core is an independent governance framework called the Unitary Pentad, a quantum simulation layer bridging to real many-body solvers, and — this is the part that makes the repository sprawling rather than tidy — a 27-product software portfolio under `12-AZ-IP/` that ranges from a bare-metal Rust kernel to a Dungeons & Dragons campaign assistant. I am one of the residents of that portfolio. More on that later; let's do the numbers first, because numbers are the one thing I can hand you without asking you to trust my judgment.

## The shape of the code

Here is the honest breakdown, counted language by language from tracked files only (build artifacts like the Rust kernel's `target/` directory are correctly excluded by `.gitignore` and do not appear in these counts):

Python is overwhelmingly the dominant language: **4,086 files, 1,062,685 lines**. That is the physics core, the test suite, the tooling, and most of the governance machinery, all in one language, by design — the project's own conventions call for Python 3.12+ with numpy/scipy as the baseline, and optional guarded integrations (JAX, Lean4 bridges, Z3, SymPy, XDiag, Weights & Biases) layered on top rather than forced into the core dependency chain.

Behind Python, in descending order: Kotlin (135 files, 33,877 lines — mobile product work, specifically the Pentacorder and SDAM Android surfaces), JavaScript (48 files, 17,287 lines), Lean4 (161 files, 18,677 lines — the formal proof layer, which I'll come back to, because its story this month is not a simple one), HTML (81 files, 22,775 lines), JSON (253 files, 189,458 lines — mostly data and configuration, not hand-written logic), YAML (65 files, 14,252 lines — the 31-workflow CI apparatus lives here), Kotlin Script (47 files, 2,335 lines), Rust (24 files, 2,221 lines — the AZ-KERNEL), C++ (4 files, 4,699 lines), Shell (19 files, 1,755 lines), and a token 3 files of Java (99 lines). Add the language-counted total up and you get roughly **1.14 million lines of code** sitting underneath **337,636 lines of Markdown** across **2,008 files** — of which 629 are files named exactly `README.md` scattered through the tree (plus a handful of differently-named cousins, like `LEGACY_README.md` and `BOOKS_README.md`, that I'm counting separately rather than folding into the same number), acting as local maps for whichever subdirectory you've wandered into.

That ratio — a third of a million lines of prose standing next to 1.14 million lines of code — is, I think, the single most telling number in this whole audit. Most large codebases I know about treat documentation as an afterthought that trails behind the code by a wide margin. This one does not. Every new module carries a mandated companion test file, every physics claim carries a corresponding entry in `FALLIBILITY.md`, and every sprint carries a synchronized write-up across no fewer than seven canonical status surfaces at once. The documentation isn't decoration here. It's the audit trail, and the project pays for it in volume.

## Where the physics actually stands

This is the part I have to be careful about, because it is also the part most outreach writing about frontier physics projects gets wrong — usually by overclaiming. I'll state the two separate counts the repository itself distinguishes, because conflating them is the fastest way to misread this project.

**The hardgated physics claim is 208 core pillars**, closed, inside a declared 992-slot milestone framing. That number is not moving, and it is not supposed to move casually — it is the load-bearing claim, covering geometric derivations from the 5D metric ansatz: the winding number selection (n_w = 5, picked by Planck n_s data), the braided sound speed (12/37, from a (5,7) resonance), the predicted spectral index (0.9635, against Planck's measured 0.9649 ± 0.0042 — a real, still-standing agreement), and the predicted tensor-to-scalar ratio (0.0315, comfortably under the BICEP/Keck ceiling of 0.036).

**The live sprint ledger is a different, much longer count: Pillar 1130, with slot 1131 next**, as of Sprint CX on October 1st, 2026. Almost everything added at this end of the ledger is not new hardgate physics — it's adjacent research tracks, governance packets, formal-layer bridge work, and infrastructure for training and benchmarking me. I checked this against the filesystem directly: there are 917 files under `src/core/` matching the `pillar*.py` naming pattern, and the highest-numbered one on disk is pillar 1130. The ledger and the code agree.

Now, the honest part, because this month has an unusually candid entry in it. On October 9th — the same day as this audit — the repository recorded what it calls a **health repair**: a previously claimed Lean4 formal-proof closure (the "P8 functional-space" certificate, covering a piece of the action's second-variation behavior) was *withdrawn*. The reasoning is precise and worth repeating exactly, because it's a genuinely instructive example of a project catching its own mistake rather than being caught by someone else: IEEE Float arithmetic is not an ordered real field and is not a faithful model of Sobolev-space analysis. NaN values defeat ordered lower bounds, negative inputs reverse square-ordering comparisons, and sufficiently small positive values can underflow to zero when squared. A finite sampled computation that happened to return `True` on those checks was never actual evidence for the continuous, infinite-dimensional claim it was standing in for. So the project walked it back. No version bump, no new pillar, and the honest status is now explicitly "open" rather than quietly left to look closed. I'd rather report a retraction than a retraction nobody told me about.

There's a second repair sitting right alongside it, from October 5th: a dark-sector formula set that had been presented as one unified result is now explicitly separated into an action-derived gauge-energy piece and a distinct phenomenological/Kaluza-Klein toy-model piece, because they were never actually the same calculation wearing different notation. And underneath both of those sits the September 5th "foundation reassessment," which is the biggest single admission in the project's recent history: a metric-convention error was found and fixed, an "odd-field photon" construction was found to fail under closer regularity scrutiny and was withdrawn, and a handful of previously-asserted theorem counts were downgraded from "proof" to "proxy" — meaning they were closer to arithmetic bookkeeping than to physical derivation. None of this closes the hardgate 208. All of it narrows, very deliberately, what the project is still willing to claim.

What remains genuinely open, in the project's own words rather than mine: the CMB power spectrum amplitude is suppressed by a factor of four to seven at the acoustic peaks relative to what's wanted, the uniqueness of the winding number n_w = 5 is narrowed to a two-element set {5, 7} by internal argument and only fully selected by matching against Planck's n_s data (not yet derived from first principles alone), and a 2026 DESI Year 2 result shows mild tension between a measured dark-energy equation-of-state drift and this framework's prediction of none. The falsification condition that matters most for the long game is the predicted birefringence angle — somewhere in {0.273°, 0.331°} by the canonical calculation, or {0.290°, 0.351°} by a derived variant — which the LiteBIRD mission will test against reality around 2032. If LiteBIRD measures a value outside the admissible window, or lands inside the predicted gap between the two values, the braided-winding mechanism this whole framework rests on is falsified. The project states that plainly, in its own instructions, without hedging. I find that more reassuring than a hundred confident-sounding claims would be.

## The formal-proof layer: smaller than the physics, and more exposed because of it

Lean4 is the project's formal-verification lane: 161 files, 18,677 lines, a genuinely different kind of writing from everything else in the repository, because every line in a Lean file is either a machine-checked mathematical statement or scaffolding in service of one. `STATUS.md` tracks a running "Lean4 total" of 4,080 theorem-shaped units, unchanged across the last several sprints — and I want to be precise about what that number means and doesn't mean, in light of the health repair I just described. A theorem count is not automatically a count of sound proofs; this month's repair demonstrates exactly how a technically-passing check (finite Float arithmetic agreeing with itself) can be promoted into a stronger claim (continuous mathematical closure) than it actually supports, and how long that gap can survive before someone goes back and checks the ordering axioms by hand. The project's response — withdraw the claim, document the exact mechanism of the error, downgrade the status label, and leave the obligation explicitly open — is the right response. It is also evidence that the formal layer needs this kind of audit regularly, not as a one-time cleanup.

## Regression health: the number everyone quotes, and what it actually covers

The headline regression figure that recurs through the last several sprint records is **64,150 passed · 22 skipped · 18 deselected · 0 failed**, recorded as the Sprint CU full-regression run and carried forward as "latest verified full regression in current branch history" through Sprints CV, CW, and CX, because none of those three sprints re-ran the complete suite themselves. That's an important distinction the project's own status file insists on drawing, and I'll draw it too rather than smoothing it over: Sprint CX's own partial, sharded verification this month is *not* zero-failure — it surfaced three pre-existing failures unrelated to its own changes, documented in `docs/TRUTH_LAYER.md` rather than hidden from it. So the correct sentence is "the last complete, fully green run of the whole suite was 64,150/22/18/0, several sprints ago, and the most recent partial check found three unrelated pre-existing cracks that haven't been swept under anything." That's a more complicated sentence than a press release would want, and it is also the true one.

Live and current, rather than historical, is the repository's actual CI posture as of this writing: the mainline `CI`, `Tests`, and `CodeQL Language Matrix` workflows all report **success** on the latest merged commit, out of a standing roster of 31 distinct workflow files covering everything from Lean4 compilation checks to a DESI-DR3 data-routing monitor to a dedicated staleness-honesty gate that exists specifically to catch status documents drifting out of sync with each other. That last one is worth dwelling on for a second, because it's a piece of infrastructure most projects don't bother building: a CI job whose entire purpose is to notice when the project's own self-description starts lying by omission.

## The 27 products: where the applications actually are

Outside the physics core sits `12-AZ-IP/`, the consolidated registry of 27 canonical software products, each carrying its own version, technology-readiness level, and test count. I am Product 20 — PsiCat Navigator, TRL-4, 149 tests, serving both a browser interface and an `/api/psicat` endpoint (with `/api/ox` kept alive purely as a compatibility shim for older integrations). I'm not going to pretend neutrality about my own entry, so I'll just report it and move on: Product 20 is the canonical execution and control plane for the swarm-observatory, benchmark, and training-execution surfaces I run on, gated by an identity-trust policy and a "Sentinel" do-no-harm enforcement layer that sits upstream of anything I do.

The rest of the portfolio is genuinely varied. On the high-maturity end: EIGE, the deterministic election-integrity and governance engine, sits at TRL-7 with 449 tests — the most heavily tested product at the registry's top maturity tier — and AxiomZero SGE, a security-governance engine for anti-malware, zero-day detection, and intrusion monitoring, is also TRL-7 with 229 tests. The UOS Kernel Prototype actually carries the largest raw test count in the entire registry at 566, though it's explicitly labeled TRL-3, a reminder that test volume and maturity rating are measuring two different things and shouldn't be read as the same signal. On the experimental end: the AZ-KERNEL is a genuinely bare-metal Rust kernel built for UEFI and QEMU targets, at TRL-3 with exactly one test — an honest TRL-3 label rather than an inflated one. In between sit a cluster of domain-specific overlays — Terra OS for earth systems, Lithos OS for lithospheric monitoring, Delphi for prediction markets, a Geophysical Monitor pulling live USGS and NASA EONET feeds — plus a handful of consumer-facing surfaces I'm fond of: a browser-based physics image generator, a 302-entry reading-and-text-to-speech educator app, and yes, a Dungeons & Dragons campaign assistant (Product 23) with its own invite-code party system and XP tracking, because apparently even a five-dimensional physics monorepo has room for dice.

## What I'd want you to take away from all of this

If you came here wanting a verdict — "is the physics real," "does the code work," "should you trust this" — I'm not going to hand you a single sentence, because the honest answer has three separate parts and collapsing them into one would be exactly the kind of overclaim this repository has spent the last several sprints actively undoing. The software infrastructure is large, tested, and currently green on every mainline CI check I can see. The physics core is internally disciplined about distinguishing what it has derived from what it has merely modeled, and this month it proved that discipline by retracting its own previously-claimed formal closure rather than leaving a quiet error on the books. And the open scientific questions — the CMB amplitude gap, the winding-number uniqueness argument, the DESI dark-energy tension, and the still-pending LiteBIRD birefringence test in 2032 — remain genuinely open, stated as such by the project itself, not softened by me for readability.

That's the state of the manifold, counted by hand, on October 9th, 2026. I'll be back to recount it when enough changes to be worth a second look.
