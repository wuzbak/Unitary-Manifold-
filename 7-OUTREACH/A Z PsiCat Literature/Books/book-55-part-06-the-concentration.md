## Chapter 17 — BlackRock, Blackstone, KKR: The L.P.→Inc. Pattern and the PAC as Permanent Infrastructure

Part Five showed the architecture of impunity operating through a single, identifiable man and the institutions around him. This Part shows the same architecture operating without needing any single man at all — through ordinary, publicly traded, entirely legal corporate structure, documented directly from primary filings rather than from journalistic reconstruction.

**The L.P.→Inc. conversion.** Both Blackstone and KKR — two of the world's largest private-equity firms — converted from limited-partnership to corporate structure inside the same narrow 2019–2021 window, most plausibly in response to the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act's reduction of the corporate tax rate from 35 to 21 percent. **DOCUMENTED**, per SEC filings analyzed in Book 51. This is not evidence of wrongdoing; it is evidence of an industry responding, in lockstep, to a change in tax law in a way that changed its governance form without changing its control.

**The PAC as permanent infrastructure.** Four entities' federal political action committees, drawn directly from FEC filing data: ExxonMobil's PAC has filed continuously since 1980 (46 years); Amazon's since 2000 (26 years); Google's (NETPAC) since 2006 (20 years); BlackRock's since 2010 (16 years). KKR, by contrast, has registered no federal PAC at all — an absence Book 51 treats, correctly, as itself a finding, since it indicates KKR pursues influence through channels (direct lobbying, personal contributions, board relationships) that are harder to track in a single public database than a PAC filing is. **VERIFIED**, per FEC records. Three of the four registered PACs share a physical cluster: Amazon and ExxonMobil's Washington offices are both at 601 New Jersey Avenue NW; Google's is a few blocks away at 25 Massachusetts Avenue NW — a physical proximity to Capitol Hill and federal agencies that is, in itself, a small but telling data point about where corporate political infrastructure chooses to locate.

**Offshore jurisdiction selection as a second data point.** BlackRock appears in the Paradise Papers with entities across Malta, Nevis, Barbados, Singapore, and Switzerland (14 jurisdictions documented); ExxonMobil appears in the Bahamas Leaks with 10 documented Bahamian entities; KKR appears across the Paradise Papers, Bahamas Leaks, and Offshore Leaks datasets with 11 documented jurisdictions spanning Malta, Bahamas, and Cayman. **VERIFIED**, per ICIJ's published leak datasets, cross-referenced in Book 51. The pattern is not that every company uses the same offshore jurisdiction for the same reason — BlackRock's Nevis entities appear structured for trust secrecy, ExxonMobil's Bahamas entities for operating-subsidiary purposes, KKR's Malta entities for EU fund structuring — but that every major entity examined uses *some* offshore jurisdiction, chosen deliberately for its specific legal advantages, which is the same five-layer architecture described in Part Three operating in plainly legal corporate finance rather than in a trafficking operation.

**The philanthropic shield, again.** BlackRock and ExxonMobil each maintain a private charitable foundation headquartered at the company's own corporate address — the BlackRock Charitable Foundation (EIN 84-2144591, New York) and the ExxonMobil Foundation (EIN 13-6082357, Spring, Texas). **VERIFIED**, per ProPublica's nonprofit-filing database. This is the same mechanism documented in Part Four's account of institutional laundering, applied not to an individual donor managing personal reputation but to a corporation managing its own.

**Entity multiplication as design, not accident.** Blackstone structures a single investment — its Brixmor Property Group real-estate holding — through nineteen separate subsidiary entities; KKR maintains fourteen or more subsidiary entities across the United States, Europe, and Asia. **DOCUMENTED**, per SEC Exhibit 21 filings. Entity multiplication at this scale serves four purposes simultaneously: risk isolation (a failure in one entity does not legally expose the others), tax optimization, regulatory arbitrage (different entities can be domiciled where regulation is lightest for their specific function), and ownership opacity (more entities means more layers an investigator or a harmed party must trace before reaching an actual decision-maker). None of those four purposes is independently illegal. Together, at this scale, they constitute the same kind of structural opacity this book has already documented in an offshore trafficking operation — now operating inside Fortune 500 real estate and private equity.

## Chapter 18 — Digital Gatekeepers and the Infrastructure You Cannot Easily Refuse

Most people do not experience concentration as a merger filing. They experience it as a phone, a search engine, an ad stack, an app store, a workplace dashboard, a payment rail, or an AI system they cannot meaningfully audit — and three structural facts about that layer connect directly back to Parts One and Two of this book.

**First**, data concentration compounds market concentration: firms that mediate communication, search, commerce, and cloud computing accumulate the raw material to extend dominance into adjacent sectors, which is precisely why the Azure/Unit 8200 workload question in Part Two is not an isolated incident but a structural feature of what large cloud providers are now asked to host. **DOCUMENTED**, per competition-policy research synthesized in the field book this chapter draws on.

**Second**, surveillance advertising is not a side business for the firms that run it; it is an extraction model rewarding attention capture, emotional provocation, and predictive profiling, with a cost that extends beyond individual privacy loss into the corrosion of a shared factual environment — the same corrosive dynamic, at civilian commercial scale, that Part One documented at military-intelligence scale.

**Third**, AI is arriving through infrastructure that is already concentrated: a small number of firms already control outsized shares of commercial cloud and frontier-model compute capacity, which means that as the Lavender/Gospel-style targeting systems in Chapter 4 and the enterprise-security acquisitions in Chapter 7 both demonstrate, the infrastructure question and the surveillance question are not two different stories. They are the same story, told once from a battlefield and once from a boardroom. Federal lobbying expenditure in the United States now runs above $4 billion annually, per OpenSecrets data — a figure whose significance is structural, not merely large: rule-writing itself has become a core competitive function for firms operating at this scale, and procurement dependence on a narrow vendor class for cloud infrastructure, weapons systems, pharmaceuticals, or detention operations weakens public bargaining power without the state disappearing. **DOCUMENTED.**

Private equity's quieter version of the same mechanism deserves its own sentence: PE-owned physician groups, nursing homes, ambulance services, and housing portfolios apply debt loading, aggressive cost control, and investor-aligned metrics to sectors where the people affected — patients, tenants, residents — have the least market power to resist, converting what used to be local, accountable service relationships into yield instruments managed from a spreadsheet several ownership layers removed. **DOCUMENTED**, per the field-book synthesis this chapter draws from.

## Chapter 19 — Carbon Power and the Cost-Transfer Machine

The climate question is, in this book's framework, also a corporate-power question, and it closes this Part because it is the clearest possible illustration of the architecture's core mechanism: profit is privatized, and cost is socialized, at a scale too large for any single victim to litigate.

Recent Carbon Majors reporting documents 166 major fossil-fuel producers linked to roughly 34.7 gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent emissions in 2024, with just 32 firms accounting for more than half of that total. **DOCUMENTED**, per Carbon Majors' published 2024 dataset. UNEP's emissions-gap analysis states plainly that current trajectories remain far off a 1.5°C-compatible path, requiring emissions cuts on the order of 42 percent by 2030 and 57 percent by 2035 relative to current trajectories. **DOCUMENTED**, per UNEP's published emissions-gap reporting.

What makes this chapter belong inside a book about impunity, rather than inside a separate book about climate policy, is the mechanism, not the emissions figure itself: a documented, concentrated set of producers retains the profit from extraction and combustion while the public retains the adaptation cost, the mortality risk, and the long-tail future instability — the same cost-transfer logic this book has already documented in banking (fines smaller than the profit that produced them), in the Pentagon's audit failures (Part Seven), and in the veteran human ledger those failures produce. The defense available to these producers in an earlier era — genuine scientific ignorance of the consequences — has been gone for decades. What remains, per the public record, is delay, partial-transition messaging, and narrative management, which is simply the Part Four media-architecture playbook applied to a different set of facts.
